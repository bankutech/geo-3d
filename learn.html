Introduction to
Geometry

The mathematical study of shape, size, and spatial relationships — from ancient clay tablets to modern theoretical physics.

What is Geometry?

From Ancient Greek geo- "earth" and -metron "measurement", geometry arose across early cultures as a practical way to handle lengths, areas, and volumes. Today it is a vast field underpinning our understanding of the universe — from the curvature of spacetime to the pixels on this screen.

History

Ancient Roots

Around 300 BC, Euclid organised geometric knowledge into "The Elements" — 13 books that remained the definitive textbook for over 2,000 years. Descartes later fused geometry with algebra through coordinate systems.

Applications

Everywhere Around Us

Architecture, engineering, computer graphics, GPS navigation, robotics, and Einstein's general relativity are all built on geometric principles. Every 3D model on this site is rendered using geometric transformations.

Branch

Euclidean

Geometry on flat planes. Parallel lines never meet. The geometry of everyday experience.

Branch

Non-Euclidean

Geometry on curved surfaces where parallel lines can meet or diverge — spherical and hyperbolic geometry.

Branch

Analytic

Geometry expressed through coordinates and algebraic equations, enabling exact computation of shapes.

Branch

Differential

Uses calculus to study smooth curves and surfaces. Forms the mathematical backbone of general relativity.

Collinear Points

Three or more points lying on the exact same line. Any two points are always collinear; three or more may or may not be.

Coplanar Points

Any three non-collinear points define a unique plane. Four or more points are coplanar only if all lie in the same plane.

Acute

Between 0° and 90°. All angles of an equilateral triangle are acute at exactly 60°.

Right

Exactly 90°. Perpendicular lines form right angles. Denoted with a small square symbol at the vertex.

Obtuse

Between 90° and 180°. Greater than a right angle but less than a straight line.

Straight

Exactly 180°. Formed when two rays point in exactly opposite directions along the same line.

Reflex

Between 180° and 360°. The "major" angle on the other side of the arms.

Complete

Exactly 360°. A full rotation back to the exact starting direction.

Lines & Relationships

How multiple lines interact in two-dimensional space — from perfectly aligned parallels to the rich angle geometry created by a transversal.

Line Relationships

Interactive

Select Relationship

Two lines that never meet, maintaining equal distance at all points. They have identical slopes when expressed in coordinate geometry.

Co-interior Angles

Between parallel lines on the same side of a transversal. Always supplementary — sum to 180°.

Alternate Angles

On opposite sides of a transversal between parallel lines. Always equal — the Z-angle pattern.

Corresponding Angles

In matching positions when a transversal crosses parallel lines. Always equal — the F-angle pattern.

Vertically Opposite

Formed across the intersection point of two lines. Always equal — directly proved by straight angle = 180°.

Triangles

Three-sided polygons and the most structurally stable shape in geometry. The Pythagorean theorem is one of mathematics' most elegant discoveries.

Pythagorean Theorem

a² + b² = c²

For any right triangle, the square of the hypotenuse equals the sum of squares of the other two sides.

By sides

Equilateral

All three sides equal. All angles are exactly 60°. Perfect 3-fold rotational symmetry.

By sides

Isosceles

Two sides equal. The base angles opposite those sides are always equal to each other.

By sides

Scalene

No sides equal. All three angles are different. No lines of symmetry exist.

Angle Sum Property

In any Euclidean triangle, the interior angles always add to exactly 180°. No exceptions.

Square

Four equal sides, four right angles. Most symmetric quadrilateral. Area = a².

Rectangle

Four right angles, opposite sides equal. Area = l × w.

Rhombus

Four equal sides, opposite angles equal. Area = ½d₁d₂ (half product of diagonals).

Parallelogram

Opposite sides parallel and equal. Area = base × perpendicular height.

Trapezium

Exactly one pair of parallel sides (trapezoid). Area = ½(a+b)h.

Kite

Two pairs of adjacent equal sides. Diagonals perpendicular. Area = ½d₁d₂.

Convex Polygons

All interior angles less than 180°. All vertices point outward. Any segment between two interior points stays fully inside.

Concave Polygons

At least one interior angle greater than 180°. The polygon "caves in" — a line between two points may exit the boundary.

A connected portion of a circle's circumference. A minor arc is less than a semicircle; major arc is greater.

Sector

Secant

An extended line passing through the circle at two distinct points, unlike a tangent which touches at one.

Mensuration

The branch of mathematics dealing with measurement of length, area, surface area, and volume of geometric figures.

What is Mensuration?

Mensuration is the branch of geometry dealing with the measurement of geometric figures. It forms the mathematical foundation for construction, manufacturing, computer graphics, and spatial analysis across all engineering disciplines.

1D

Perimeter

The continuous boundary length of a closed figure. Measured in linear units (cm, m, km).

2D

Area

The extent of a surface region. Measured in square units (cm², m²).

2D

Surface Area

Total area of all surfaces of a 3D object when unfolded flat. Square units (cm², m²).

3D

Volume

The amount of space enclosed by a 3D object. Cubic units (cm³, m³, litres).

Real-World Applications

Architecture

Calculating material quantities — tiles, paint, concrete — for construction projects of any scale.

Engineering

Designing parts with precise volumes and fluid capacities; calculating stress distribution across surfaces.

Computer Graphics

Bounding boxes, collision detection, and surface shading all rely on mensuration formulas in real time.

Manufacturing

Optimising packaging materials and container shapes to minimise material waste and shipping costs.

Transformations

Mathematical operations that change the position, size, or orientation of geometric figures while preserving specific properties.

Transformation Explorer

Interactive

Isometric Transformations

Transformations that preserve shape and size — translation (slide), rotation (turn), and reflection (flip). Congruent figures result.

Non-Isometric Transformations

Transformations that change size but preserve shape — enlargement (dilation). Similar figures result, not congruent.

Symmetry

A figure has line symmetry if it maps onto itself under reflection. Rotational symmetry exists if it maps onto itself under rotation by less than 360°. A circle has infinite lines of symmetry and infinite-order rotational symmetry.

Geometry in Technology

How ancient mathematical principles drive modern innovation — from pixels on your screen to satellites in orbit.

🎮

Computer Graphics & Games

Every 3D game uses mesh geometry, matrix transformations, and projective geometry to render 3D environments onto 2D screens in real time at 60+ frames per second.

🏗️

CAD & Architecture

Computer-Aided Design relies on Bézier curves, NURBS splines, and solid modelling to design everything from smartphone cases to skyscrapers with submillimetre precision.

🤖

Robotics & Kinematics

Inverse kinematics — calculating joint angles to reach a target position — is built entirely on trigonometry and spatial geometry. Every robotic arm uses it.

🌐

GPS & Navigation

Global Positioning Systems use spherical geometry and trilateration to pinpoint your exact location from satellite distances, accounting for Earth's curved surface.

🧠

Machine Learning

Neural networks operate in high-dimensional geometric spaces. Concepts like manifold learning, nearest-neighbour search, and attention all use geometric intuition.

🔬

Medical Imaging

CT and MRI reconstruction algorithms use Radon transforms and geometric back-projection to build 3D models from 2D cross-sectional scans.

The Geometry of This Website

Every 3D solid rendered on this site uses Three.js, which applies rotation matrices, perspective projection, and Phong lighting models — all pure geometry. The 3D viewer above uses quaternion-based camera control derived from spherical geometry. Mathematics is not abstract here — it is running live in your browser.
